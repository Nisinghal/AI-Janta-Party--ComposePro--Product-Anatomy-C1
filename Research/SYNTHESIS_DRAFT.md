## Clusters

### C1. The gap is vocabulary, not effort

Tension: sensing something is wrong doesn't automatically turn into knowing what to change.
What they did: named exact fixes for things they'd learned before (overhead lighting, a familiar pose problem) but stalled on anything novel, describing it only as "flat," "cramped," or "off," and in one case couldn't tell at all until a bigger screen or another person pointed it out.

- n07 — Could name most problems exactly (hand, eyes closed, stranger) but not the lighting one, just knew the colors looked "flat and washed out." [Maya-Q4]
- n17 — Usually can't articulate what's off, has a vague sense of "not great" without knowing if it's lighting, angle, or something else. [Derek-Q4]
- n23 — Held the phone out (needs reading glasses to see the screen clearly), looked fine at first; only noticed the backlighting (sun behind Emma, face dark) when reviewing on a bigger screen at home. [Linda-Q2]
- n25 — Could not self-diagnose the lighting issue; her daughter had to point it out. If her daughter hadn't said anything, she'd have kept the first shot. [Linda-Q4]
- n31 — Disliked it immediately: overhead kitchen light cast harsh under-eye shadows on Raj, white cabinets blew out totally white. [Priya-Q2]
- n33 — Knows overhead lighting's effect on faces precisely and avoids it, but sometimes a photo "feels cramped" or off in a way she can't name, and she'll reposition without being able to explain why. [Priya-Q4]
- n41 — Almost always knows exactly what's wrong, thinking in specific technical terms (horizon line, catchlight in the eyes, jaw shadow); occasionally something feels off and takes a moment to isolate, e.g. realizing a background pole appears to grow out of someone's head. [Jordan-Q4]

### C6. Fixing a shot is trial and error, one variable at a time

Tension: there's no single correction, only a sequence of retakes, each one addressing exactly one thing.
What they did: retook the shot multiple times, fixing one specific issue per attempt, whether or not they could name it, rather than correcting everything in one pass.

- n03 — 2 of 6 were blurry from subject movement. [Maya-Q2]
- n04 — 1 of 6 had a stranger walking through the background. [Maya-Q2]
- n05 — In most of the rest, subject's hand covered the jacket, the point of the photo. [Maya-Q2]
- n06 — Retook 4 more times: hand moved, waited out a cloud (lighting), recentered the mural, subject blinked. [Maya-Q3]
- n24 — Took 2 more shots on-site after her daughter told her to reposition away from the backlighting. [Linda-Q3]
- n32 — Retook 3 times: had Raj step away from the overhead light, switched to under-cabinet lighting, redid it because the daughter looked away. [Priya-Q3]
- n40 — Retook 6 times: first for a tilted horizon line, then had the subject shift weight to her back foot (more flattering than square-on), then twice more waiting for hair not to blow across her face. [Jordan-Q3]

### C2. Directing the subject ranges from natural to uncomfortable

Tension: giving a person instructions on how to stand or hold their face isn't a neutral act, it costs something socially for some people.
What they did: three gave detailed, specific direction without hesitation; one skipped it outright because it felt "weird" and "precious" for a casual photo.

- n08 — Gave explicit direction: drop hand, turn shoulders off-axis, chin down slightly to avoid an odd neck angle. [Maya-Q5]
- n18 — Never gives direction to subjects, feels "weird"/"precious" about it for a casual snapshot. [Derek-Q5]
- n26 — Gave direction: hold trophy higher, smile with the whole face not just the mouth (a habit Emma has). [Linda-Q5]
- n34 — Gave direction: had Raj angle his body away from straight-on; told her daughter to look at Raj instead of the camera, since a candid glance photographs better than a forced smile at that age. [Priya-Q5]
- n42 — Gives direction constantly and in detail: drop one shoulder toward camera, turn chin down/toward the light, weight on back leg, even cued the exact moment to exhale so shoulders relax. [Jordan-Q5]

### C3. Nobody catches their own blind spot

Tension: everyone has one habitual mistake, and not one of them found it themselves.
What they did: kept repeating the same error until a roommate, sibling, daughter, or friend pointed it out by name.

- n09 — Roommate has repeatedly told her she stands too far back and crops in, making photos softer; she does this out of fear of cutting off feet. [Maya-Q6]
- n19 — Sister has told him he consistently cuts off the top of people's heads. [Derek-Q6]
- n27 — Daughter regularly tells her she shoots from too high up, making people look shorter; she still forgets in the moment. [Linda-Q6]
- n35 — Sister told her she shoots too close with the main lens, causing wide-lens facial distortion, and to step back and zoom instead, she does this now. [Priya-Q6]
- n43 — A photographer friend told him he centers subjects too much and should use the rule of thirds instead, the advice "stuck." [Jordan-Q6]

### C4. The favorite photo is always the one nobody directed

Tension: fixing the technical problems and getting a photo people actually love are not the same task.
What they did: in every single interview, the best-loved past photo was unposed and undirected, credited to a real laugh or a moment of motion rather than anything the photographer engineered.

- n10 — Favorite past photo (rooftop bar, July): no direction given, subject candid mid-laugh talking to someone else. [Maya-Q7]
- n11 — Felt different because it wasn't "engineered", light did the work, expression was real. [Maya-Q7]
- n20 — Favorite past photo: Marcus laughing at his phone, unposed, at a barbecue, attributes it to a lucky angle and even shade instead of harsh overhead sun. [Derek-Q7]
- n28 — Favorite past photo: husband holding new grandson on the porch swing, Easter, late afternoon light, no posing or direction, came out soft and warm, faces relaxed. [Linda-Q7]
- n36 — Favorite past photo: daughter running toward Raj, arms out, beach, golden hour, fully candid, nobody looking at the camera. [Priya-Q7]
- n44 — Favorite past photo: same trip, Sam mid-laugh after a dumb joke, completely unplanned, no direction, happened "the second I gave up trying" after 20 minutes of posing. [Jordan-Q7]

### C5. Giving up looks like settling, not stopping

Tension: the shot doesn't stop because it's good, it stops because patience runs out.
What they did: kept the least-bad option once retakes stalled, almost always to avoid holding up the group, not because they were satisfied.

- n12 — When running late, uses the least-bad shot and says "this one's fine" without fully believing it. [Maya-Q8]
- n13 — Has abandoned a shot once or twice when the subject said "forget it, let's go." [Maya-Q8]
- n21 — Uses the bad photo anyway for group chats: "nobody cares that much except maybe the person in it." [Derek-Q8]
- n29 — If it's not working after 2-3 tries, keeps the best available rather than making a fuss, so as not to hold up the group. [Linda-Q8]
- n37 — After 3-4 unsuccessful retakes, keeps the least-bad option rather than prolonging it; occasionally abandons entirely, telling herself she'll get it another day. [Priya-Q8]
- n45 — Rarely skips a shot, keeps going until the light or the subject's patience runs out; if truly unfixable, deletes rather than keeping a known-bad photo. [Jordan-Q8]

## Two plain lines per cluster

**C1**
What they're trying to get done: know what to change when a shot feels wrong.
What gets in the way: without technical vocabulary (lighting, angle, composition) or a way to see the flaw live, the feeling of "off" doesn't turn into an actionable fix.

**C2**
What they're trying to get done: get their subject into a better position or expression.
What gets in the way: for some, giving direction feels socially awkward, so they skip it even when they sense it would help.

**C3**
What they're trying to get done: avoid their own recurring mistakes.
What gets in the way: they can't see their own habitual errors; every fix traceable to a mistake came from someone else pointing it out, never self-discovered.

**C4**
What they're trying to get done: capture a photo that feels genuine, not stiff.
What gets in the way: active guidance and direction, even when well-intentioned, seems to produce technically correct but less-loved photos than candid, undirected moments.

**C5**
What they're trying to get done: get a good photo without dragging out the moment.
What gets in the way: patience (theirs or their subject's) runs out before quality does, so they keep the least-bad option rather than chase a genuinely good one.

**C6**
What they're trying to get done: turn a "something's wrong" moment into a finished shot.
What gets in the way: there's no way to fix everything at once, so each retake addresses exactly one thing, and the shot only converges after several rounds.

## Three candidate problem statements

**A.** People sense something is wrong with a portrait but lack the vocabulary to fix it, and that gap tracks experience, not how much they care. Trial and error is the workaround: fixing one thing per retake, whether or not they can name it.
Covers: C1, C3, C6. Leaves out: C2, C4, C5.
Against it: close to the original scope-card guess, almost word for word. Doesn't explain why fixing the technical problem doesn't produce the photo people actually love (C4), or why people quit before they're satisfied (C5).

**B.** Real-time guidance can fix the things people can name, but the photos people love most happen specifically when nobody is directing anything.
Covers: C1, C3, C4. Leaves out: C2, C5, C6.
Against it: argues against the core bet almost directly. If a tool that actively tells someone what to change produces technically correct but less-loved photos, "build a guidance app" may be solving for the wrong outcome entirely.

**C.** People don't run out of problems, they run out of patience, and the fixes available (self-taught rules, a companion who happens to notice) arrive too slowly to matter before the moment collapses.
Covers: C1, C3, C5. Leaves out: C2, C4, C6.
Against it: doesn't touch the discomfort of coaching a subject (C2), and doesn't address the fact that even a faster fix might still just produce a faster technically correct but unloved photo (C4).
